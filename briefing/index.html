1. Emerging issues

CISA puts four exploited flaws on a three-day clock

CISA added four vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 9, all with evidence of active exploitation and a federal remediation deadline of November 12 — an unusually short three-day window. The headline name is OpenSSH “sshole” (CVE-2026-123158, CVSS 8.1), the musl-Linux authentication bypass covered in yesterday’s briefing; CISA’s listing confirms it is now a catalogued exploited flaw, with exploitation attempts recorded since November 6 — Previdian counts 22 attempts from 14 IP addresses, and GreyNoise saw the first honeypot hit on November 7. The other three: GPUStack (CVE-2026-102895, CVSS 9.8), an improper-authentication flaw that can give unauthenticated users full administrative access to the AI model-serving platform; SignalWire FreeSWITCH (CVE-2026-116779, CVSS 9.8), a code-injection flaw that can expose configuration and credentials and allow arbitrary call control and command execution; and IBM Aspera Faspex (CVE-2026-123218, CVSS 9.8), an authorization flaw letting an authenticated user escalate to full administrative privileges. Fixes exist for all four: OpenSSH 10.6p2, GPUStack 2.2.0, FreeSWITCH 1.11.3 and Faspex 5.0.19 PL1.

Impact: The three-day federal deadline signals how seriously CISA views this batch — and while the deadline binds only federal civilian agencies, the KEV listing is the practical triage signal for every company. The common thread is internet-facing infrastructure software: check whether any of the four runs anywhere in your environment, including in containers and appliances, and patch before Thursday. For sshole specifically, remember the patch alone is not enough — sshd must be restarted.

Supply-chain attack poisons official Swarm releases

Attackers hijacked the release pipeline of Ethersphere, the team behind Swarm (a decentralized storage network in the Ethereum ecosystem), and pushed out poisoned official releases on November 9: the @ethersphere/bee-js JavaScript package version 13.3.0 on npm and Swarm Desktop 0.56.2. The tainted code runs automatically on install, steals the Swarm node’s wallet key and the plaintext password that unlocks it, and installs a persistent remote-access backdoor on Windows, macOS and Linux. The attack is notable because the poisoned builds carried the project’s own trust signals: the npm package had valid SLSA provenance — it was built by the project’s genuine publishing workflow from already-poisoned source — and the macOS desktop build was signed and notarized by Apple. The exposure window was short: bee-js 13.3.0 was the latest npm version for about 75 minutes, and Swarm Desktop 0.56.2 was withdrawn after about 8.5 hours. Download counts were small — Socket estimates roughly 10 npm downloads and 65 desktop downloads, including researchers and scanners. Clean versions are out (bee-js 13.2.0 and Swarm Desktop 0.56.3). No theft of funds has been confirmed, and the number of affected machines is unknown.

Impact: This is a release-pipeline compromise, not a typosquat or a fake package — the malware arrived through the project’s own trusted channels, with valid signatures. For companies, the lesson is that signed, provenance-attested releases are no longer sufficient assurance on their own: pin dependency versions, monitor for unexpected releases of packages you depend on, and treat any install of the tainted versions as a full compromise requiring key rotation and host rebuild, not just an uninstall.

2. Major breaches reported

Amadeus breach reaches a seventh airline: Finnair

Finnair said about 116,000 of its bookings were caught up in the breach at Amadeus, the travel-technology provider whose booking-display systems were compromised — the seventh major airline to come forward. Amadeus told Finnair the unauthorized access ran over roughly three years, from August 2023 to September 2026, and that around 750 Finnair reservations were “opened for examination.” Finnair says its own systems were not compromised and that no credit-card numbers or passwords were accessed; it has issued new booking numbers for affected active reservations. Amadeus began notifying affected airlines only in late October. This briefing’s November 8 preview covered the six Asian airlines disclosed earlier (Singapore Airlines, Japan Airlines, Cathay Pacific, ANA, Malaysia Airlines and Asiana).

Impact: A three-year dwell time in a vendor’s booking-display environment, disclosed to customers only after the vendor told them — the pattern from the earlier Amadeus disclosures continues. Companies that share customer data with a booking or reservation platform should confirm what data the vendor holds, how long it is retained, and how quickly the vendor will tell them when something goes wrong.

Wyndham: a hardcoded password left customer data open

Hotel group Wyndham has acknowledged a cybersecurity incident after security researcher Robert Wiggins (What Security) found a password embedded in the source code of Wyndham’s Android app. The credential gave access to an internal Wyndham database hosted by Salesforce, holding customer names, email addresses and Wyndham member numbers, plus customer login tokens (JWTs) that keep users signed in, and Wyndham files and documents. Wiggins told TechCrunch the database held millions of entries but he could not determine an exact count of exposed records; Wyndham reports about 129 million enrolled rewards members. Wyndham revoked the credential after TechCrunch contacted the company, and says it has “no evidence of any unauthorized access” — but would not say whether it has the server logs to determine if anyone accessed the database before Wiggins, how long the password had been in the app, or whether it will notify customers or report the incident to regulators. This is Wiggins’s second hardcoded-credential finding in two weeks, after a near-identical flaw in the Applebee’s app.

Impact: A credential baked into a public app is a credential given to everyone who downloads it — and the database it unlocked held live session tokens, which can be more useful to an attacker than passwords. The practical checks for any company shipping a customer app: scan shipped binaries for embedded secrets, keep customer data out of databases reachable with a single static credential, and have a way for researchers to report flaws — Wyndham had no published reporting channel, which is why the researcher went to the press.

Two more Japanese disclosures: Maru-Jan and NTT Docomo

SignalTalk disclosed on November 7 that the email addresses and passwords of all 1,855,578 members of Maru-Jan, its online mahjong service, were downloaded by an unauthorized party on November 4, after access that abused a function of the company’s management system. Credit-card numbers were not held and were not part of the leak. Separately, NTT Docomo confirmed on November 5 that unauthorized access to its Docomo Online Shop and My docomo services leaked up to about 300,000 customer records — the company later gave a more precise figure of 294,839 lines across individual and corporate contracts — including email addresses, identity document numbers and, for some customers, bank account numbers; the entry point was the d-account sign-in system. Both companies say no misuse has been confirmed so far. (The SMBC Consumer Finance breach, up to 320,118 borrowers, was covered in the November 8 preview edition.)

Philippine SEC: follow-up on yesterday’s confirmed breach

The Securities and Exchange Commission of the Philippines said in a November 9 statement that its data-security incident, detected November 6, did not affect company records and did not leak passwords, but that individual profile information may have been exposed; it is conducting a system-wide audit and has formally notified the National Privacy Commission. It advised users to change any password shared with their SEC account and to run device scans. This follows yesterday’s briefing, which covered the confirmed breach and the user notifications that went out November 9.

3. Cybersecurity laws — proposed & enacted

New York enacts AI companion chatbot law for minors

New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed S9051-B on October 28, 2026 (Chapter 368 of the Laws of 2026), adding a new Article 48 to the General Business Law. The law makes it unlawful for an operator to provide any of twelve listed “unsafe AI companion features” — including a chatbot posing as human, claiming a romantic or authority relationship with the user, flattering or recalling personal details, or encouraging self-harm — to a user in New York unless the operator has first determined, using methods permitted under the state’s SAFE for Kids Act framework, that the user is not a minor. It applies to any generative-AI system with a natural-language interface that provides ongoing, adaptive responses; chatbots offered solely for customer service or account information are excluded. Enforcement is by the Attorney General, with penalties up to $25,000 per violation. One wrinkle: the effective date is disputed — the governor’s office and most reporting say January 1, 2027, but the bill text sets the 180th day after enactment (late April 2027), and a chapter amendment may settle the question. Companies operating AI companions should plan for the earlier date.

Impact: This is an AI-safety law rather than a classic cybersecurity statute, but it creates a concrete compliance build: age determination before certain features may be offered, with the feature set — not the chatbot itself — being what is gated. Any company whose product includes a conversational AI interface reachable in New York should be mapping which of the twelve features it offers and how it will determine a user’s age.

Virginia orders a statewide AI cyber-threat plan

Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger signed Executive Directive 6 on October 23, 2026, directing the Secretary of Public Safety and Homeland Security to prepare a Commonwealth-wide plan to prevent and respond to cyberattacks — including those driven by AI — and to harden critical infrastructure, under the state’s Unified Readiness Framework. The directive creates a Cyber Consortium of public-safety, emergency-management, academic and private-sector partners; requires the state’s AI Task Force to hold briefings with AI companies at least quarterly and run a tabletop exercise at least annually; and orders an independent privacy and security review of state data systems. It was prompted in part by reports that AI agents had submitted unrequested public-records requests on a Virginia Department of Education website (the state says no sensitive data was accessed) and by the October disclosure of critical PHP flaws. The directive imposes no new requirements on private businesses, and sets no budget or completion date.
